HVAC Proposal Checklist: What to Review Before You Sign

Review the complete project scope—including equipment, ducts, electrical work, permits, exclusions, warranties, and commissioning—before signing.

A system proposal can look complete while leaving out the details that determine whether the finished project actually solves the problem.

A low price may exclude electrical work, duct modifications, permits, equipment removal, or startup testing.

A better HVAC proposal checklist helps homeowners evaluate the work being performed, not only the price at the bottom of the page.

For a heat pump, furnace, air conditioner, mini-split, or full system replacement, the proposal should make clear:

  • what the contractor found;
  • what system is recommended;
  • what work is included;
  • what work is excluded;
  • who is responsible for each part of the project;
  • how the completed system will be verified.

You should not have to discover necessary project details after the installation date is already on the calendar.

Start With the Problem the Proposal Is Solving

A useful proposal begins before the equipment model numbers.

It should identify the reason for the project.

That may include:

  • aging equipment;
  • repeated repairs;
  • rooms that do not stay comfortable;
  • limited cooling;
  • high operating cost;
  • an addition or ADU;
  • failed equipment;
  • conversion from gas to electric heating;
  • replacement of both heating and cooling equipment.

Look for notes about the existing system and the home.

That may include:

  • equipment type and location;
  • accessible duct condition;
  • return-air concerns;
  • electrical capacity;
  • thermostat location;
  • drainage;
  • venting;
  • refrigerant piping;
  • known airflow problems.

Not every proposal needs to become a long engineering report.

However, it should show that the recommendation came from an assessment of the actual house rather than from a standard equipment package.

This becomes especially important when changing system types.

A gas-furnace-to-heat-pump project, for example, may affect:

  • electrical work;
  • backup heat;
  • condensate drainage;
  • outdoor-unit placement;
  • refrigerant piping;
  • controls;
  • duct airflow.

A proposal that simply says “replace furnace” does not answer those project-specific questions.

HVAC Proposal Checklist: Scope Before Price

The scope of work is the center of a good proposal.

It should explain what the contractor will:

  • furnish;
  • install;
  • modify;
  • remove;
  • connect;
  • test.

If an item is essential to the finished system, it should not be left to an assumption.

Review whether the proposal clearly identifies:

  • the indoor equipment;
  • outdoor equipment;
  • quantity of units;
  • major accessories;
  • thermostat or control system;
  • equipment removal;
  • disposal;
  • duct transitions;
  • return-air connections;
  • filter cabinets;
  • line sets and refrigerant piping;
  • condensate drains;
  • flues where applicable;
  • gas piping where applicable;
  • electrical connections;
  • disconnects;
  • breakers where required;
  • mounting hardware;
  • equipment pads or stands;
  • penetrations through walls, roofs, or exterior surfaces;
  • sealing of penetrations;
  • permits;
  • inspection fees;
  • startup;
  • commissioning;
  • homeowner walkthrough.

The exact scope depends on the house and the equipment.

For example, reusing an existing refrigerant line set can be reasonable in some replacements.

Reuse should depend on:

  • condition;
  • compatibility;
  • required size;
  • cleanliness;
  • accessibility;
  • manufacturer requirements.

It should not be based only on convenience.

The same applies to ductwork.

Full duct replacement is not automatically necessary, but a system conversion may require:

  • new transitions;
  • improved return air;
  • sealing;
  • balancing;
  • selected branch modifications;
  • trunk modifications.

The proposal should state what is planned.

Confirm Equipment Details and Capacity

A proposal should identify the equipment clearly enough for the homeowner to know what is being purchased.

At minimum, confirm:

  • manufacturer;
  • model number;
  • system type;
  • nominal capacity;
  • relevant efficiency ratings;
  • major matched components;
  • controls.

“High-efficiency heat pump” is a description.

It is not a complete equipment selection.

Capacity deserves particular attention.

Bigger equipment is not automatically better.

Excessive oversizing can contribute to:

  • short cycling;
  • uneven room temperatures;
  • less stable comfort;
  • weaker moisture removal during cooling.

Undersized equipment may be unable to maintain the desired indoor conditions during design weather.

For major replacement projects, equipment sizing should be based on the home's actual heating and cooling load and the performance of the selected equipment.

Existing-system history is useful context, but it should not be the sole sizing method.

For heat pumps in the Puget Sound region, ask:

  • how the proposed equipment performs at colder outdoor temperatures;
  • whether backup or auxiliary heat is included;
  • how the existing ducts were evaluated;
  • whether the indoor and outdoor equipment are an approved combination.

For ductless systems, confirm which rooms or areas each indoor unit is intended to serve.

For furnace-and-AC systems, verify compatible indoor and outdoor components.

Look Closely at Ductwork and Airflow Work

Many comfort complaints are air-distribution problems rather than equipment problems.

A new heat pump cannot automatically correct:

  • a restricted return;
  • an undersized branch;
  • poor airflow balancing;
  • a damaged duct;
  • a poorly located supply register.

If the project is intended to improve hot or cold rooms, the written scope should identify the planned solution.

Possible work may include:

  • adding a return;
  • resizing a return;
  • modifying supply runs;
  • sealing accessible ductwork;
  • improving transitions;
  • adjusting dampers;
  • balancing airflow;
  • correcting damaged flexible duct.

If ductwork is not included, the proposal should say so clearly.

Be cautious of broad statements such as:

“Improve comfort throughout the home”

when there is no defined plan explaining how that improvement will be achieved.

Some homes have construction or access limitations that prevent every room from reaching identical temperatures.

A dependable contractor should explain those trade-offs before approval.

Separate Firm Pricing From Allowances

The total project price should be easy to find.

However, line items, assumptions, and exclusions matter too.

Review whether the price includes:

  • labor;
  • equipment;
  • materials;
  • tax;
  • disposal;
  • permits;
  • inspections;
  • electrical work;
  • ductwork;
  • startup;
  • commissioning.

Some items may be listed as allowances.

Examples can include:

  • electrical upgrades;
  • concealed wiring conditions;
  • carpentry;
  • crane access;
  • specialty materials;
  • hidden structural conditions.

An allowance is not automatically a warning sign.

Some conditions cannot be fully verified until equipment is removed or an electrical panel is inspected in more detail.

What matters is that the proposal explains:

  • what is known;
  • what remains uncertain;
  • what allowance is included;
  • what could change the final price.

Ask how changes are approved.

If unexpected work becomes necessary, the contractor should provide a written change order describing the added scope and cost before proceeding whenever practical.

Clear estimates and clear change documentation protect both the homeowner and the contractor.

Check Permits, Rebates, and Project Documentation

Permits and inspections may be required depending on the project and jurisdiction.

The proposal should state:

  • who obtains required permits;
  • who coordinates inspections;
  • whether permit fees are included;
  • whether separate electrical work requires another contractor or permit.

Do not assume that a neighbor's HVAC project followed the same requirements.

If the project may qualify for:

  • a utility rebate;
  • manufacturer promotion;
  • financing program;
  • other incentive;

ask what documentation the contractor will provide.

Eligibility can depend on:

  • exact equipment;
  • installation date;
  • program rules;
  • utility territory;
  • household circumstances;
  • contractor documentation.

Incentives should not be presented as guaranteed money before eligibility is confirmed.

Keep project documents together:

  • proposal;
  • equipment information;
  • permits;
  • inspection records;
  • final invoice;
  • warranty registrations;
  • rebate documentation.

These records can be useful for future service, warranty questions, home sales, and insurance documentation.

Understand Warranties and Who Stands Behind the Work

Manufacturer equipment warranties and contractor labor or workmanship coverage are different.

A manufacturer may cover eligible equipment parts under stated terms.

Contractor coverage addresses installation-related labor or workmanship according to the contractor's own policy.

The proposal should explain:

  • manufacturer warranty;
  • contractor warranty;
  • registration requirements;
  • important exclusions;
  • whom to contact if a concern develops.

Also ask who will perform the actual installation.

That continuity reduces the chance that important field conditions or homeowner priorities disappear during a sales-to-installation handoff.

Do Not Skip Startup and Final Testing

Installation day is not finished when the equipment turns on.

A proposal should include final testing appropriate to the system.

Depending on the equipment, commissioning can include:

  • verifying refrigerant-system setup;
  • checking operating temperatures;
  • confirming airflow;
  • checking drainage;
  • confirming controls;
  • testing safety functions;
  • thermostat setup;
  • startup and shutdown verification.

The contractor should also explain basic operation and maintenance.

Homeowners should know:

  • how to use the controls;
  • how to access or replace filters;
  • how ductless filters are cleaned where applicable;
  • what normal operation looks like;
  • what maintenance is recommended;
  • whom to call if a problem appears.

Compare Proposals on Equal Terms

When two proposals have very different totals, compare the project scope rather than only the price.

Look at:

  • equipment;
  • capacity;
  • efficiency;
  • ductwork;
  • electrical scope;
  • controls;
  • permits;
  • equipment removal;
  • commissioning;
  • warranties;
  • exclusions.

One contractor may include work another contractor has left for later.
