HVAC Maintenance Checklist for Puget Sound Homes

What to check yourself each month and season, what a professional visit should include, and the signs that mean you should call before the next scheduled check.

A missed filter change can turn a quiet heating or cooling system into a noisy, expensive service call. This HVAC maintenance checklist separates the simple tasks worth handling yourself from the work that needs a trained technician. The goal is not to take your equipment apart. It is to keep airflow clear, catch changes early and give the system a better chance of delivering even, dependable comfort.

Puget Sound homes put their equipment through a lot. Damp winters, spring pollen, summer smoke events and long run times on heat pumps and furnaces each load filters, outdoor units, drains and ductwork differently. A short routine prevents many avoidable problems, but it does not replace a proper professional inspection.

The Tasks Homeowners Can Safely Handle

Most useful maintenance is simple and visible. None of it involves refrigerant, gas piping, electrical panels or combustion parts. Set a recurring reminder instead of waiting until the system seems to have a problem.

Check and replace the air filter

A restricted filter is one of the most common causes of weak airflow, extra equipment noise and uneven rooms. Check it monthly while the system is in regular use. Depending on the filter type, household size, pets, construction dust and wildfire smoke, it may need replacing every one to three months.

Use the size and airflow direction printed on the existing filter or cabinet. A higher MERV rating is not always better: a filter that is too restrictive for your blower can cut airflow and strain the system. Our guide to choosing HVAC filters for allergies explains how to balance filtration and airflow, and a technician can confirm what your equipment handles.

Keep supply and return vents open and clear

Walk through the house and look at each register and return grille. Furniture, rugs, curtains and storage bins block more airflow than most homeowners realize. Keep vents open unless a professional has given you a specific balancing plan, because closing several vents to push air into one room raises static pressure and makes the system work harder.

A return grille needs room to pull air back to the equipment. Vacuum visible dust from grilles with a brush attachment, but do not push tools or cleaning products into the ducts. If some rooms stay stuffy even with clear grilles, see how to improve return airflow.

Look over the outdoor unit

A heat pump or central air conditioner needs open space to move air across its coil. Clear leaves, branches and plants around the cabinet, keep the clearances in the manufacturer instructions and never stack items on top or cover the unit with a solid tarp while it can run. In our wet climate, a unit raised on a pad or stand drains rain and defrost water far better than one sitting in mud.

In fall and winter, light frost that clears during a defrost cycle is normal. Heavy ice that stays, grinding sounds, repeated shutdowns or a house that will not stay warm are not; see normal heat pump frost versus a service problem. Do not chip ice off the coil.

Check the thermostat and how the system behaves

Confirm the thermostat has the correct time, schedule and mode, and replace its batteries if it uses them. When you change the setpoint, the system should start and deliver conditioned air after its normal delay. Listen for new rattles, squeals, buzzing or frequent starts and stops; rapid cycling is covered in our guide to thermostat-related short cycling.

Notice whether one room has become much warmer or cooler than the rest of the house. It may be the filter, but it can also point to duct leakage, a damper problem, insulation, system sizing or an equipment fault. Telling the technician when it happens saves diagnostic time.

Watch for water where it does not belong

High-efficiency furnaces, air conditioners, heat pumps and mini-splits all produce condensate. Check around indoor equipment for water, staining or a musty smell. If you see an active leak, turn the system off if it is safe to do so and arrange service: water usually means a clogged drain, a failed pump or an installation issue, and it damages finishes quickly.

Seasonal HVAC Maintenance Checklist

You do not need to do every task every month. Use this schedule as a reminder list, and pay the most attention just before each busy season.

  • Every month in use: check the filter; glance at the outdoor unit; listen for new noises; check around indoor equipment for water.
  • Fall, before heating season: replace the filter; clear leaves from the outdoor unit; test heat mode; test carbon monoxide alarms if you have any gas appliances; book professional maintenance.
  • Winter: keep the outdoor unit clear of leaves and snow; watch for ice that does not clear; note whether backup or auxiliary heat seems to run constantly.
  • Spring, before cooling season: replace the filter after pollen season; clear the outdoor cabinet; test cooling mode and confirm air moves at every register.
  • Summer and smoke events: check filters more often; replace any visibly loaded filter right away.
  • Once a year: professional inspection and cleaning of each system, including mini-split indoor heads.

Before the heating season

Before relying on a furnace or heat pump for daily heat, inspect or replace the filter, clear the outdoor unit and test the thermostat in heat mode. For gas furnaces, make sure carbon monoxide alarms are installed and working. If you smell gas, see soot, notice a yellow burner flame or a CO alarm goes off, leave the home and call your gas utility or 911. Do not diagnose combustion or gas problems yourself; our guide to furnace safety hazards explains the warning signs.

Heat pump owners should also watch backup heat. If the system runs almost constantly, blows cooler air than usual or leans on auxiliary heat more than before, schedule an assessment. The cause may be settings, airflow, refrigerant charge or the house itself.

Before the cooling season

When temperatures rise, check the outdoor cabinet and the area around it, confirm the thermostat can call for cooling and feel for air at the registers. Do not spray high-pressure water into the coil fins or open electrical panels; when a coil really does need cleaning, see when coil cleaning helps. A system that runs but does not cool well is worth checking in spring, before the first hot week fills every contractor's schedule.

During smoke and pollen periods

Wildfire smoke, heavy pollen, remodeling and nearby construction can load a filter in days. Replace it as soon as it looks dirty, especially with variable-speed equipment that runs for long stretches. Media cabinets, electronic air cleaners and humidifiers have their own maintenance schedules, so include them in your professional visit.

What Professional HVAC Maintenance Should Include

A professional visit is more than a filter change. The exact scope depends on whether you have a gas furnace, a ducted heat pump, a mini-split, central air or a combination, but it should always be a hands-on inspection with performance checks, not a quick look at the thermostat.

For a ducted system, expect a technician to check electrical connections, blower operation, safety controls, condensate drainage, accessible duct connections, thermostat operation, temperature change across the equipment and airflow. Heat pump and air conditioning maintenance adds coil condition, refrigerant-circuit performance, defrost operation and outdoor-unit components. Furnace maintenance should include inspection of combustion-related parts and venting by a qualified professional.

Mini-splits need their own attention

Indoor heads collect dust, pet hair and moisture on the coil and blower wheel, where a filter rinse cannot reach. A head that smells musty, drips, blows weakly or has dark specks on the vanes usually needs a deep cleaning: the indoor head is opened up, the blower wheel and coil are cleaned, the drain area is cleared and the system is tested after reassembly. You can see what that looks like in our mini-split deep cleaning project in Seattle.

A good technician explains what was found in plain language. If a repair is recommended, ask what it affects now, what could happen if it waits and whether there is more than one reasonable option. Written findings help when you are deciding between a repair, a duct correction or planning for replacement.

Signs to Schedule Service Sooner

Maintenance does not prevent every failure. Call for a diagnostic visit between scheduled checks if you notice:

  • a sudden rise in energy use without a weather explanation;
  • rooms that no longer heat or cool evenly;
  • rapid cycling, new noises or a breaker that trips;
  • water around indoor equipment or a drain that keeps backing up;
  • ice that stays on a heat pump's outdoor unit;
  • burning smells, gas odors, soot or a carbon monoxide alarm.

Some of these are urgent; others can wait for normal business hours. Gas odors and CO alarms always come first: leave the home and call for help. For everything else, describe the symptom, when it started and whether the system still runs. That helps the technician prepare and makes diagnosis faster.

Maintenance Records Make Replacement Decisions Easier

Regular maintenance builds a record of how your equipment performs as it ages. If repairs become frequent, comfort is inconsistent or the system cannot keep up with an addition or remodeled space, the conversation may shift from maintenance to replacement planning.

That does not automatically make replacement the right answer. A straightforward repair is often sensible for equipment with years of life left; our guide on when HVAC repair makes sense walks through the decision. Keep a simple log of filter changes, service visits, repairs and unusual behavior. When a technician assesses your home, those details turn a vague complaint into a clear plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should HVAC maintenance be done?

Check the filter monthly while the system is in regular use and replace it every one to three months, more often during wildfire smoke or construction. Schedule professional maintenance once a year for a furnace or air conditioner, and once a year for a heat pump or mini-split that heats and cools year-round. Mini-splits with heavy use, pets or musty odors may need a deep cleaning more often.

What HVAC maintenance can a homeowner do?

Homeowners can change or rinse filters, keep supply and return vents clear, clear leaves and debris from around the outdoor unit, check thermostat settings and batteries, and watch for water, ice, odors or new noises. Anything involving refrigerant, gas, electrical panels or combustion parts should be left to a licensed technician.
