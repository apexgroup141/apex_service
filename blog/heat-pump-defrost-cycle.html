How the Heat Pump Defrost Cycle Works

Defrost is a normal operating sequence that temporarily warms the outdoor coil so a heat pump can continue heating efficiently.

An air-source heat pump collects heat from outdoor air during winter. Because the outdoor coil becomes cold, moisture can condense and freeze on its surface. A light coating of frost restricts airflow over time, so the system needs a controlled way to remove it.

The defrost cycle is that controlled sequence. It is not the same as a breakdown, and understanding what happens can keep normal sounds, steam, and temporary changes in indoor airflow from becoming a surprise.

How Frost Forms During Heating

In heating mode, the outdoor coil functions as the evaporator. Refrigerant absorbs heat outdoors and carries it inside. When the coil surface is below freezing and outdoor air contains moisture, frost begins to form on the fins.

Puget Sound’s cool, wet weather can create favorable conditions for frost even when the air temperature is above freezing. The system is designed to manage a limited amount of frost rather than prevent every crystal from appearing.

How the System Initiates Defrost

Heat pumps use different defrost-control strategies. Some systems use time-and-temperature logic, while others use demand-defrost controls that monitor operating conditions and initiate defrost when needed.

The exact inputs vary by equipment. Controls may use coil temperature, outdoor temperature, compressor runtime, pressure-related information, or proprietary algorithms. Defrost should occur often enough to restore airflow without operating longer or more frequently than conditions require.

Reversing Refrigerant Flow

When defrost begins, the reversing valve changes the refrigerant circuit so heat is directed to the outdoor coil. This temporarily makes the system operate in a cooling-like mode even though the thermostat still calls for heat.

The compressor typically continues operating. The warm refrigerant raises the outdoor coil temperature and melts the frost. The sequence is controlled; homeowners should not try to force it by repeatedly changing thermostat modes or cycling power.

What the Outdoor Fan Does

Many systems stop the outdoor fan during defrost. With the fan stopped, heat remains at the coil instead of being carried away by outdoor airflow. The fan restarts when the system returns to normal heating.

You may hear a change in sound as the reversing valve shifts. A visible cloud can also rise from the outdoor unit as meltwater evaporates from the warm coil. Brief steam during defrost is normal and should not be confused with smoke.

What Happens Indoors

Because the refrigerant circuit has reversed temporarily, the indoor coil is not delivering normal heat during the defrost portion of the cycle.

Some ducted systems use supplemental heat to reduce the amount of cool air delivered indoors. Other systems may slow or stop the indoor fan temporarily. The exact behavior depends on the equipment and controls.

Ductless mini-splits may pause or change indoor fan operation during defrost. With multi-zone equipment, the response can vary by manufacturer and configuration.

Returning to Normal Heating

Once the controls determine that defrost is complete, the reversing valve returns to heating position and the outdoor fan resumes. Water drains from the coil and base, and the indoor system returns to its normal heating response.

The complete cycle is usually brief. Frequency and duration depend on outdoor temperature, humidity, equipment design, coil condition, airflow, and control strategy.

Drainage Is Part of Defrost Performance

Every defrost cycle creates water. The outdoor unit needs a stable base, appropriate clearance, and a route for drainage. Roof runoff, leaves, snow, or a low spot around the pad can allow water to refreeze near the coil or fan.

Outdoor-unit placement and drainage should be considered during installation and verified during startup. Our HVAC commissioning guide explains why final system checks matter beyond simply confirming that warm air reaches a register.

When Defrost Is No Longer Normal

Arrange professional service when:

  • Frost does not clear.
  • Defrost cycles repeat unusually often or continue unusually long.
  • The outdoor fan contacts ice.
  • Indoor comfort drops significantly and does not recover.

A light frost that clears during normal defrost operation is expected. Thick ice that remains on the coil or interferes with the fan is not. For causes of persistent icing, see our guide to why heat pumps frost and when service is needed.

A technician can verify sensors, control sequence, reversing-valve operation, fan operation, coil condition, drainage, and refrigerant performance when appropriate.

Normal defrost is one of the ways a heat pump protects its winter performance. It should clear the coil, drain the meltwater, and return the system to heating without homeowner intervention.
