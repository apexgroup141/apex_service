Whole House Ventilation Guide for Puget Sound Homes

Compare exhaust-only, supply-only, balanced, HRV, and ERV ventilation while planning filtration, controls, duct integration, and final balancing.

A house can have a capable heat pump, a new furnace, and well-sealed windows yet still feel stuffy in the bedrooms or hold cooking odors longer than expected.

That is where whole-house ventilation becomes important.

Whole-house ventilation is not about making the home colder during winter or running every bathroom fan at full speed all day.

It is a planned method of exchanging indoor and outdoor air at a controlled rate.

For Puget Sound homeowners, ventilation often becomes part of the conversation during:

  • a remodel;
  • addition;
  • ADU project;
  • new construction;
  • HVAC replacement;
  • air-sealing project.

Better insulation and air sealing improve energy performance, but they also reduce the incidental air leakage that older homes may have relied on.

The right ventilation approach should consider:

  • the home;
  • occupancy;
  • building tightness;
  • ductwork;
  • HVAC system;
  • outdoor conditions;
  • code requirements;
  • how the occupants will actually use the system.

What Whole-House Ventilation Is Designed to Do

Whole-house ventilation provides controlled air exchange.

Depending on the design, the system can:

  • exhaust indoor air;
  • supply outdoor air;
  • do both in a balanced system.

This is different from spot ventilation.

Examples of spot ventilation include:

  • bathroom exhaust fans;
  • kitchen range hoods.

Those systems address moisture, cooking products, or odors in a specific area for a limited period.

Whole-house ventilation manages background air exchange across the home.

The goal is controlled ventilation rather than an intentionally drafty house.

This distinction also matters when homeowners compare HVAC equipment.

A high-efficiency filter can capture particles from air that passes through the HVAC system.

It does not automatically bring outdoor air into the house.

Likewise, a heat pump can heat and cool efficiently but is not automatically a whole-house ventilation system.

Heating, cooling, filtration, and ventilation can work together, but they serve different functions.

Main Types of Whole-House Ventilation

The best option depends on:

  • building layout;
  • climate;
  • existing HVAC system;
  • available duct routes;
  • home tightness;
  • project scope;
  • budget.

An existing home with limited access may require a different approach from new construction where ventilation routes can be planned before finishes are installed.

Exhaust-Only Ventilation

An exhaust-only system uses one or more fans to remove indoor air.

Fans may operate continuously at a lower rate, intermittently, or through designed controls depending on the system.

Replacement air enters through designated inlets where provided and through available building leakage paths depending on the design.

This can be a practical and relatively simple approach in some homes.

The trade-off is that incoming air is generally less controlled than in a dedicated supply or balanced system.

Pressure relationships also matter.

Exhaust systems can create negative pressure inside the home.

That means ventilation design should consider:

  • fireplaces;
  • combustion appliances;
  • large exhaust fans;
  • other equipment affected by building pressure.

Supply-Only Ventilation

Supply-only systems use a fan to introduce outdoor air into the home.

Outdoor air may be delivered:

  • through dedicated ducts;
  • into a central HVAC distribution system;
  • through another designed supply location.

Incoming air can be filtered before entering the occupied space.

Depending on airflow and building leakage, a supply system can create slight positive pressure relative to outdoors.

This approach provides more control over where outdoor air enters.

However, the design still needs a clear strategy for how indoor air leaves the building.

Supply-only ventilation is not automatically the best choice for every home or moisture condition.

Balanced Ventilation With HRV or ERV

Balanced systems supply outdoor air while exhausting a similar amount of indoor air.

Many use either:

  • HRV — Heat Recovery Ventilator;
  • ERV — Energy Recovery Ventilator.

These systems use a heat-exchange core to transfer energy between separate incoming and outgoing airstreams.

The two air streams remain separated through the core.

During winter, the system can use energy from outgoing indoor air to temper incoming outdoor air.

During warmer weather, energy exchange can reduce part of the load associated with outdoor ventilation air.

An ERV also transfers some moisture through its core.

An HRV primarily transfers sensible heat.

Neither HRV nor ERV is automatically the correct choice for every Puget Sound home.

Selection depends on:

  • moisture profile;
  • occupancy;
  • building tightness;
  • indoor comfort concerns;
  • HVAC design;
  • ventilation load;
  • maintenance needs.

Duct routing, accessibility, balancing, controls, and commissioning matter just as much as the equipment label.

How Ventilation Is Sized and Planned

Ventilation should not be selected from square footage alone.

Home size matters, but the design can also depend on:

  • number of bedrooms;
  • occupancy;
  • layout;
  • ceiling heights;
  • building characteristics;
  • applicable ventilation standards;
  • local code requirements.

A system that is too small may not provide the intended ventilation rate.

An oversized system can create:

  • unnecessary noise;
  • drafts;
  • higher operating cost;
  • pressure issues.

During the assessment, the contractor should consider where air is:

  • supplied;
  • exhausted;
  • transferred between rooms.

Bedrooms deserve attention when doors are closed.

The design should also consider whether the home uses:

  • central ducted HVAC;
  • ductless equipment;
  • a combination of both.

Integrating Ventilation With Central HVAC Ductwork

In some homes, ventilation air can be integrated with the existing central duct system.

That approach requires the duct system to be suitable for the intended airflow and distribution.

Integration also needs a control strategy so ventilation airflow is delivered as intended when the central HVAC blower is on or off, depending on the design.

In other projects, dedicated ventilation ducts provide more predictable distribution.

The correct approach depends on:

  • duct layout;
  • equipment;
  • controls;
  • available space;
  • service access.

A ductless mini-split provides excellent zoned heating and cooling.

It generally does not distribute outdoor ventilation air throughout the home by itself.

That does not make ductless HVAC a poor choice.

It simply means that ventilation requires its own design.

Building Pressure and Exhaust Equipment

Kitchen range hoods, bath fans, dryers, fireplaces, and combustion appliances can affect the pressure relationships inside a home.

Large exhaust appliances can depressurize the building.

Ventilation design should therefore consider how these systems operate together.

This is especially important in homes containing combustion appliances.

Whole-house ventilation should be designed as part of the building system rather than as an isolated fan.

Filtration, Noise, and Controls Matter

A ventilation system only helps when homeowners are comfortable leaving it in operation.

A system that is:

  • noisy;
  • difficult to control;
  • difficult to maintain

is more likely to be shut off.

Filter selection deserves attention.

Higher-efficiency filters can capture smaller particles, but airflow resistance depends on the specific:

  • filter design;
  • filter size;
  • depth;
  • face area;
  • airflow rate;
  • loading.

Filter rating alone does not determine pressure drop.

The ventilation equipment, filter cabinet, blower, and ductwork need to be compatible with the selected filter.

Controls should also be practical.

Depending on the equipment, controls can include:

  • continuous low-speed operation;
  • boost mode;
  • timers;
  • occupancy settings;
  • humidity-responsive controls.

The homeowner should understand what each setting does before the project is completed.

Maintenance Access Is Part of the Design

Ventilation equipment needs future service access.

That can include access to:

  • filters;
  • HRV/ERV cores;
  • condensate drains where applicable;
  • fans;
  • controls;
  • balancing points.

Hiding equipment in an extremely difficult attic location may look clean on installation day but create unnecessary difficulty every time the unit requires maintenance.

Serviceability should be part of equipment placement.

When to Add Ventilation to an HVAC Project

Whole-house ventilation is easiest to coordinate during:

  • new construction;
  • major remodel;
  • addition;
  • ADU project;
  • full HVAC replacement.

Walls and ceilings may already be open.

Electrical and drainage requirements can be coordinated before finishes are completed.

Ventilation can also be added to an existing home.

The practical question becomes:

What approach provides the needed air exchange without unnecessary disruption?

Some projects require:

  • limited new ductwork;
  • dedicated ventilation ducts;
  • compact equipment;
  • carefully selected routes through attics, crawlspaces, closets, or utility areas.

If a furnace is being replaced or a heat pump is being installed, it is a good time to review whether the existing duct system can support the planned ventilation strategy.

Existing room comfort problems should also be documented.

Airflow balancing cannot solve every ventilation problem, but it can reveal whether the existing distribution system contributes to uneven room conditions.

Questions to Ask Before Approving a Ventilation Proposal

A clear proposal should explain:

  • ventilation-system type;
  • equipment;
  • supply locations;
  • exhaust locations;
  • duct routing;
  • controls;
  • filters;
  • electrical work;
  • drainage where applicable;
  • permit responsibility;
  • access limitations.

Ask how airflow will be verified after installation.

A ventilation system should be tested and adjusted rather than simply mounted and turned on.

Also ask:

  • Where are the filters?
  • How are they accessed?
  • What maintenance is required?
  • How often should the homeowner inspect them?
  • How will the ventilation rate be adjusted?
  • Are permits required?
  • How will the final airflow be confirmed?

Permit and inspection requirements can vary by jurisdiction and project scope across the Puget Sound region.

The contractor should review those requirements before work begins rather than treating them as an afterthought.

The Right Ventilation System Is the One That Fits the Home

The best ventilation system is not automatically the largest or most elaborate option.

It is the system that:

  • fits the home;
  • provides the intended airflow;
  • is installed cleanly;
  • remains accessible for service;
  • is properly controlled;
  • is balanced and verified after startup.

Before selecting equipment, the home and its airflow should be reviewed as a complete system.

That gives homeowners a stronger basis for choosing a ventilation approach that continues to make sense long after installation day.
