Ducted vs. Ductless Heat Pumps: Which Fits Your Home?

Both are heat pumps. The difference is how heat reaches your rooms, and that depends on your ducts, your floor plan and how you use each space.

A heat pump decision often starts when a furnace is near the end of its life, a few rooms never feel comfortable, or summer cooling becomes hard to ignore. When comparing ducted vs. ductless heat pumps, the right answer is not simply which one is more efficient. It is which system can heat and cool your home evenly, fit the way you use each room, and be installed correctly in the house you already have.

For many Puget Sound homes, both options can work. The better fit depends on the condition of the existing ductwork, the layout of the home, whether you are remodeling or adding space, and how much you value room-by-room control versus one whole-home setting. If you are still deciding between a heat pump and central air conditioning, start with ductless vs. central air; this guide assumes you have already chosen a heat pump.

How Each System Works

A ducted heat pump uses an indoor air handler (or a furnace with a heat pump coil) connected to supply and return ducts. Air moves through floor, wall or ceiling registers, just like a central furnace. If your home has a usable duct system, it delivers the familiar central-heating experience with efficient electric equipment.

A ductless heat pump, commonly called a mini-split, uses one or more wall-mounted, ceiling cassette or concealed indoor units connected to an outdoor unit. Each indoor unit serves a defined zone and conditions the space where it is installed instead of pushing air through ducts.

Both move heat rather than burning fuel, and both heat and cool. The practical difference is distribution.

When a Ducted Heat Pump Is the Better Fit

A ducted system is often the sensible choice for a home with existing ductwork that is properly sized, reasonably sealed and able to reach the rooms that need conditioning. Equipment stays out of sight, registers look familiar and one central system serves many rooms.

Existing ducts are an asset if they are in good condition

Having ducts does not automatically mean they should be reused. Older systems may have undersized returns, disconnected runs, heavy leakage, poor insulation in a crawlspace or attic, or a layout built around an oversized furnace. Those problems can limit a new heat pump before it has a chance to perform.

A proper assessment looks beyond whether air comes out of the vents: duct condition, airflow capacity, return air, problem rooms and space for indoor equipment. Our guide Can Ducts Support Heat Pumps? walks through each check. In many homes, targeted duct sealing, a larger return or airflow balancing makes a central heat pump a strong long-term solution.

Ducted systems suit whole-home consistency

Families who want every bedroom, hallway and living area served from one control often prefer ducted equipment, especially in homes with many enclosed rooms, multi-level plans or occupants who do not want visible indoor units. It is also a natural path when an aging furnace and central air conditioner are being replaced together. Our heat pump and air handler replacement in Kirkland is a typical example.

When a Ductless Heat Pump Makes More Sense

Ductless systems solve problems that ducts cannot solve economically: homes with no ducts, homes where adding ducts would mean extensive demolition, and spaces that need independent control.

An addition, ADU, converted garage, upstairs bonus room or daylight basement is often an excellent candidate. A mini-split can serve that area without overloading an older central system. See ductless mini-splits for ADUs and our 3-zone mini-split project.

Zone control is a real advantage

Each indoor unit has its own control, so a home office can stay comfortable during the day without heating unused rooms to the same setting. It also helps rooms that run warmer or cooler because of sun, large windows or a location over a garage. Our home zoning guide compares this with zoned duct systems.

Zone control is not automatic whole-home comfort. One wall-mounted head does not reliably serve several closed bedrooms around corners or on another floor. A ductless design needs enough indoor units, in the right locations, for the spaces people actually use.

Appearance and placement deserve an honest discussion

Wall-mounted indoor units are visible. Many homeowners find modern units unobtrusive; others prefer vents. Ceiling cassettes and slim concealed ducted units are alternatives, but they change scope and cost.

The outdoor unit needs service access, proper clearances, a plan for condensate and a location that does not create noise near patios or bedroom windows. These details should be settled before installation day.

Installation Cost and Project Scope

Ductless is not automatically cheaper. In a home with no ductwork, a few well-planned zones usually cost less and disrupt less than building a full duct system. A larger home with many separate rooms, however, may need several indoor units, multiple line-set runs, electrical work and detailed finish work, and a ducted system may be more practical when a usable duct network already exists.

Reusing ducts can be economical, but only if they suit the new equipment. Attaching a heat pump to restrictive or leaky ducts leads to noise, weak airflow and uneven temperatures.

Performance Depends on Design, Not Just Equipment

A heat pump must be sized for the home’s calculated heating and cooling load, not the size of the old furnace or square footage alone. Insulation, windows, air leakage, ceiling height and sun exposure all affect the load; our heat pump sizing guide explains the process. In the Puget Sound climate, winter heating capacity still needs attention even though winters are milder than in much of the country.

With ducted systems, airflow drives performance. With ductless systems, indoor-unit placement and zone sizing drive performance. Either way, correct electrical work (see HVAC electrical requirements), refrigerant piping, condensate drainage and final commissioning decide whether the system delivers the comfort you paid for.

A Hybrid System Can Be the Practical Answer

Many homes do not need an all-or-nothing choice. A ducted heat pump can handle the main living areas and bedrooms while a ductless unit serves an addition, an ADU or a stubborn upstairs room. This keeps a good central system and fixes the specific problem without rebuilding every duct run.

A hybrid design should still be planned as one comfort strategy, with appropriate capacity, coordinated controls and a clear understanding of which equipment serves each area.

Questions to Settle Before Choosing

  • What condition are the existing ducts in, and which rooms are hardest to keep comfortable?
  • Do you want one central setting or independent room control?
  • Are visible indoor units acceptable in the main living areas?
  • Are an addition, ADU or remodel likely in the next few years?
  • How did the contractor size the system, and what duct or electrical changes are included?
  • How will the system be tested at completion, and what warranty documents will you receive?

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a ductless heat pump cheaper than a ducted heat pump?

Sometimes. In a home without ducts, a few well-planned ductless zones usually cost less and disrupt less than building a duct system. In a larger home with many closed rooms and usable ducts already in place, a ducted heat pump is often the more practical and economical choice.

Can one mini-split heat a whole house?

Rarely. One wall-mounted head conditions the open area it faces well, but it does not reliably reach closed bedrooms around corners or on another floor. Whole-home ductless designs need enough indoor units in the right places.

Can I keep my ducts and add a mini-split for one room?

Yes. A hybrid design is common: a ducted heat pump serves the main house while a ductless unit handles an addition, ADU, bonus room or a room the central system has never served well.
