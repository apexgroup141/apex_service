Furnace Replacement Cost in Washington: What Homeowners Should Expect

Understand the equipment, ductwork, venting, permit and installation factors that shape the final cost of replacing a furnace.

A furnace that quits during a cold Puget Sound week creates an immediate question: repair it, or replace it?

Furnace replacement cost depends on far more than the price printed on the equipment cabinet. The right project starts with the home, the existing duct system, fuel source, comfort concerns, and the work required to make the new system perform as promised.

For many Puget Sound homeowners, a straightforward gas furnace replacement may cost roughly $4,000 to $8,500. Projects can cost more when they involve high-efficiency equipment, difficult access, venting changes, ductwork corrections, electrical work, permits, or other upgrades.

An in-home assessment is the only reliable way to price a specific installation.

What Is Included in Furnace Replacement Cost?

A written furnace proposal should separate the equipment from the work needed to install it correctly.

A low starting price can look appealing until important parts of the job appear later as change orders. Ask what is included before comparing one estimate to another.

A complete replacement commonly includes removal and disposal of the old furnace, the new unit, installation labor, gas and venting connections, electrical connections, a new filter cabinet or transition pieces as needed, permit costs, and startup testing.

It should also include thermostat setup, safety checks, and an explanation of the new system controls.

The furnace itself is only one portion of the investment. A correctly installed furnace can provide dependable heat for years, but its results depend on proper sizing, airflow, combustion setup, venting, and commissioning.

A quality installation protects comfort and helps support manufacturer warranty requirements.

The Main Factors That Change the Price

Furnace Size and Efficiency

A larger furnace is not automatically better.

An oversized unit may heat the home quickly but cycle on and off too often, which can affect comfort, efficiency, and equipment life.

A contractor should evaluate the home’s square footage, insulation, windows, layout, ceiling height, and duct capacity rather than simply matching the old furnace’s input rating.

Efficiency also affects furnace replacement cost.

Lower-efficiency non-condensing furnaces generally have a lower installed price and may be practical where the existing venting configuration and local code requirements support them.

High-efficiency condensing furnaces cost more upfront because they require different venting and condensate drainage, but they can reduce fuel use in the right application.

The savings depend on how much heat the home needs, local utility rates, and the efficiency of the existing system.

Variable-speed blowers and two-stage or modulating furnaces can improve airflow and temperature consistency.

They are often worthwhile for homeowners dealing with cold bedrooms, loud operation, or uneven temperatures, but they are not required in every home.

The best option should fit the house and budget, not simply carry the highest feature list.

Ductwork and Airflow Corrections

A new furnace cannot solve every comfort problem by itself.

Restricted return air, undersized supply ducts, disconnected duct runs, and poor balancing can leave rooms uncomfortable even after replacement.

Minor duct transitions are normal in many replacement projects. Larger ductwork modifications add cost, but they may be necessary to protect the new equipment and improve airflow.

This is especially common when upgrading to a variable-speed furnace, adding central air conditioning, finishing an addition, or correcting a system that was improperly sized from the beginning.

An estimate should explain whether ductwork is being inspected, what changes are recommended, and why.

If a contractor identifies an airflow issue, ask whether it is required for safe operation, recommended for better performance, or optional for future improvement.

Those are different decisions and should be priced clearly.

Venting, Gas Piping, and Electrical Work

High-efficiency furnaces use sealed combustion venting and produce condensate that needs a proper drain route.

Older homes may need new PVC vent pipes, a condensate pump, drain improvements, or changes to the existing flue setup.

Gas piping may need adjustment if the new furnace has different connection requirements or if an existing line does not meet current standards.

Electrical work can include a new disconnect, outlet, circuit correction, or wiring changes for a new thermostat.

These details are not cosmetic extras. They are part of installing a safe, code-compliant heating system.

Permits and Local Code Requirements

Permits are an important part of a furnace replacement, particularly when gas, venting, electrical, or structural work is involved.

Permit requirements can vary among Tacoma, Pierce County, Seattle, King County, and nearby jurisdictions.

The proposal should state whether permit fees and inspection coordination are included.

Permit work provides a record that the installation was reviewed against applicable requirements. It also helps homeowners maintain complete documentation for future sale, refinancing, insurance, or warranty needs.

When Repair Makes More Sense Than Replacement

Replacement is not always the responsible recommendation.

A repair may be the better choice when the furnace is relatively young, the issue is isolated, replacement parts are available, and the rest of the system is in sound condition.

The decision shifts when repairs are becoming frequent, the heat exchanger is damaged, parts are obsolete, the furnace has safety concerns, or operating costs are climbing.

Age matters, but condition matters more.

A well-maintained furnace can sometimes justify repair beyond the age when another unit might need replacement.

A useful conversation includes both options: what the repair costs now, what it does and does not address, the condition of the system overall, and what a replacement would change.

Homeowners should not have to choose under pressure without seeing the numbers in writing.

Should You Replace the Furnace With a Heat Pump?

Many Puget Sound homeowners are considering heat pumps when an existing gas furnace reaches the end of its life.

A ducted heat pump provides heating and cooling from one system and can be a practical electrification option in Washington’s generally moderate climate.

The initial cost is often higher than a furnace-only replacement, particularly if the project includes an air handler, outdoor unit, electrical upgrades, or duct improvements.

Available utility, local, or income-qualified incentive programs may reduce the net project cost when funding and eligibility requirements apply.

Homeowners should confirm current program rules before counting on a specific amount.

A dual-fuel setup is another option.

It combines a heat pump with a gas furnace, allowing the system to use different heat sources based on outdoor conditions and system controls.

This approach can offer comfort and flexibility, though it adds equipment and installation cost.

Questions to Ask Before Approving an Estimate

Before selecting a contractor, make sure the proposal answers the practical questions that affect the finished job.

You should know the furnace make and model, efficiency rating, warranty terms, scope of ductwork, permit handling, and what happens to the old equipment.

Ask how the contractor determined the equipment size and whether the system will be tested for proper airflow and safe operation after installation.

It is also reasonable to ask who will perform the work.

At Apex Service Group, the professionals who assess the home and prepare the estimate also complete the installation.

That direct responsibility helps prevent important details from getting lost between a sales visit and installation day.

Be cautious with estimates that list only a furnace model and a single total.

They may not explain venting changes, filter cabinet work, thermostat compatibility, duct corrections, electrical needs, or permit costs.

A clear written proposal makes it easier to compare projects fairly and avoid surprises.

Planning for the Project

Most standard furnace replacements can often be completed in a day, while more involved installations may take longer.

Before work begins, clear access to the furnace, electrical panel, thermostat area, and affected duct locations.

If the furnace is in an attic, crawlspace, garage, or tight closet, access conditions can affect labor and scheduling.

After installation, the job is not finished when the heat first turns on.

The system should be checked for safe combustion where applicable, gas leaks, venting, temperature rise, airflow, thermostat operation, condensate drainage, and overall performance.

Keep the written proposal, permit records, warranty information, and maintenance recommendations together for future reference.

The best furnace replacement is not necessarily the least expensive quote or the highest-efficiency model.

It is the system that is sized for your home, installed cleanly, tested carefully, and explained clearly before you approve the work.
