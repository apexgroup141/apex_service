Electric Heat vs. Gas Heat: What Makes Sense for Your Home?

Compare heat pumps, resistance heating and gas furnaces around the home you own—not a one-size-fits-all fuel rule.

A furnace that is nearing the end of its life creates a decision most homeowners only make once or twice: replace gas with gas, or move to electric heat.

For Puget Sound homeowners, electric heat versus gas heat is not a simple question of which fuel costs less.

The right answer depends on the equipment type, your home's ducts and insulation, available utility incentives, electrical capacity, and whether you also need air conditioning.

The first detail that matters is often missed in broad online comparisons.

“Electric heat” can mean electric resistance heaters, an electric furnace, or a heat pump.

Those systems use electricity very differently.

A properly selected heat pump can provide efficient heating and cooling from one outdoor unit, while electric resistance equipment creates heat directly from electrical energy.

That distinction can change both the upfront price and the monthly operating cost.

Electric Heat Versus Gas Heat: Start With the Equipment

A gas furnace burns natural gas and uses a blower to distribute warm air through ductwork.

Modern high-efficiency furnaces may use two-stage, modulating, or variable-speed operation depending on the equipment, which can improve comfort and reduce some of the temperature swings associated with older single-stage systems.

A gas furnace produces noticeably hot supply air and can raise the indoor temperature quickly on a cold morning.

Electric resistance heating converts nearly all of the electricity it uses into heat at the point of use.

It can be part of an electric furnace, baseboard heaters, wall heaters, or backup heat strips inside a heat pump air handler.

It is simple and reliable, but it generally requires substantially more electricity than a heat pump to deliver the same amount of useful heating.

A heat pump is also electric, but it does not create most of its heat directly.

It moves heat from outdoor air into the home.

Depending on outdoor temperature and equipment performance, a heat pump can deliver multiple units of heat for each unit of electricity it uses.

It also reverses in summer to provide cooling.

For many Western Washington homes, this is the electric option worth comparing against a gas furnace.

Comfort Is More Than a Thermostat Setting

Gas heat and heat pump heat feel different, and neither is automatically better.

A gas furnace sends hotter air through the registers.

That can feel fast and familiar, especially in a home that gets chilly overnight or has older ductwork.

A heat pump typically delivers air at a lower temperature than a gas furnace but may run longer and more steadily.

When designed and sized correctly, that longer operation can hold rooms closer to the thermostat setting, reduce hot-and-cold swings, and improve circulation.

It should not feel uncomfortably drafty.

If it does, the issue may involve airflow, duct leakage, equipment sizing, register placement, or an installation that was never properly commissioned.

Ductless mini-splits are another electric option for homes without usable ducts, additions, garages, ADUs, or rooms that are consistently uncomfortable.

They allow heating and cooling by zone, but indoor-unit placement matters.

A single wall-mounted unit cannot always move conditioned air evenly through a closed-door floor plan.

Operating Cost Depends on Your Home and Utility Rates

Homeowners understandably ask which choice will cost less each month.

There is no honest one-line answer.

Natural gas rates, electric rates, weather, insulation levels, thermostat habits, equipment efficiency, and utility service territory all matter.

A high-efficiency gas furnace can be economical to operate where natural gas service is already in place.

However, a home may still need separate air conditioning if it does not already have it.

Replacing a furnace and an aging air conditioner at the same time can make a heat pump comparison more favorable because one system can provide both heating and cooling.

Heat pumps are particularly relevant in the Puget Sound region because much of the heating season occurs at relatively moderate outdoor temperatures.

Their capacity and efficiency change as outdoor temperatures fall, but modern cold-climate equipment can continue providing useful heat below freezing when properly selected.

On the coldest days, some ducted systems use electric heat strips for supplemental or backup capacity.

That backup heat is effective but less efficient than heat-pump operation and should not be carrying the home unnecessarily during normal winter weather.

The best way to compare likely operating costs is to review the actual home and utility use rather than relying only on a national calculator.

A contractor should consider square footage, insulation, windows, duct condition, existing equipment, fuel usage, and how the household uses different areas.

Installation Scope Can Change the Decision

Replacing a gas furnace with another gas furnace is often a more straightforward project when the gas line, venting, electrical service, and ductwork are in good condition.

That does not mean it should be treated as a blind like-for-like swap.

Furnace capacity, blower performance, filter access, condensate drainage, combustion venting, and return-air sizing still need to be checked.

Moving from gas heat to a heat pump may require additional work.

The project can include a new outdoor unit, indoor coil or air handler, electrical circuit upgrades, condensate routing, thermostat changes, and duct modifications.

If the home currently has a furnace with central air conditioning, the existing duct system may be usable, but it should be evaluated rather than assumed adequate.

Older ducts can be undersized, poorly sealed, or missing needed returns.

A new high-efficiency system cannot correct rooms that never receive enough airflow.

Before approving a replacement, ask how airflow will be checked, whether duct modifications are included in the written scope, and how the completed system will be tested.

For homes with no gas service or no central ducts, a ductless heat pump can avoid major remodeling.

For homes with complex layouts, a combination of ducted and ductless equipment may make more sense than trying to force one system to serve every space.

Safety, Maintenance, and Home Resilience

Both gas and electric systems can be safe when installed and maintained correctly.

Gas appliances involve combustion, venting, and carbon monoxide risk if equipment or venting fails.

Homes with fuel-burning appliances should have appropriately located working carbon monoxide alarms.

Furnace maintenance should include relevant combustion, venting, drainage, ignition, safety-control, and airflow checks.

Electric heat eliminates on-site combustion from the heating equipment itself.

Heat pumps still need professional care.

Clean coils, correct refrigerant circuit operation, clear condensate drainage, secure electrical connections, and proper airflow all affect performance.

Dirty filters and neglected outdoor units can increase operating costs and equipment stress regardless of fuel source.

A standard gas furnace and a standard heat pump both normally require electricity for blowers, controls, and other components.

Neither should be assumed to provide normal whole-home heat during a power outage.

If outage resilience is a priority, discuss generator sizing, electrical loads, equipment compatibility, and backup plans before choosing equipment.

Rebates and Long-Term Planning

Available rebates can materially affect the installed cost of qualifying heat-pump projects, but programs change and eligibility may depend on equipment ratings, utility territory, income requirements, funding, and installation details.

Rebate planning should be treated as part of the project rather than an afterthought after the equipment is installed.

For a deeper explanation of eligibility and timing, see our guide to Washington heat pump rebates.

It is also worth considering where the home is headed.

If you want air conditioning, have rooms that run too cold, are planning an addition, or expect to replace both a furnace and AC soon, a heat pump deserves a serious comparison. This is also the right point to evaluate heat pump installation in Bellevue around the home’s actual electrical and duct conditions.

If your gas furnace is relatively new, reliable, and paired with an AC system that still performs well, immediate replacement may not be the best use of your budget.

Questions to Ask Before You Approve a System

A clear proposal should explain the equipment being offered and why it fits the home.

Before making a decision, ask whether the contractor evaluated the home's heating needs, how duct airflow will be verified, and what backup heat is included with a heat pump when applicable.

Ask for the expected scope of electrical, duct, and thermostat work, along with warranty terms and rebate requirements.

You should also know who will perform the installation.

At Apex Service Group, the professionals who assess the home and prepare the estimate are directly involved in completing the work.

That helps keep system recommendations tied to the actual conditions found in the home rather than a generic replacement quote.

The practical choice is the one that gives your household dependable heat, manageable operating costs, and a system that has been sized, installed, and tested for the home you actually own.

A careful in-home assessment is more useful than a blanket answer, especially when the next system may serve your home for many years.
