Heat Pump Emergency Heat: When to Use It

"Emergency Heat" on the thermostat sounds alarming, and "Aux Heat" is often confused with it. Here is what each one means, when emergency heat is the right call and when it is a sign the heat pump needs a technician.

When the thermostat shows emergency heat, it can feel like a warning that something has failed. Sometimes it is. Often it is simply a backup setting doing its job. The key is telling the difference between normal auxiliary heat, emergency heat you turned on deliberately and a heat pump that needs professional attention.

For Puget Sound homeowners this matters most during a cold snap or after a power outage, when comfort is urgent and electric backup heat can add noticeably to the bill. Emergency heat is useful, but it is not a setting to leave on all winter.

What Emergency Heat Actually Does

A heat pump heats the house by moving heat from the outdoor air indoors. Even cold air holds usable heat. The outdoor unit collects it, the indoor air handler or mini-split heads distribute it, and the system uses far less electricity than resistance heat to do it.

Emergency heat tells the system to stop using the outdoor heat pump and run only its backup heat. In most all-electric systems, that backup is electric heat strips inside the air handler. In a dual-fuel system, it is the gas furnace. On the thermostat it may be labeled EM Heat, Emergency Heat or E Heat.

Electric heat strips create heat directly, much like the elements in a toaster. They are dependable, but they use a lot more electricity than the heat pump does in normal operation. A gas furnace backup has different costs, but emergency mode still bypasses the heat pump completely.

Emergency heat exists to keep the house warm when the outdoor part of the system cannot or should not run. It is not a boost for ordinary cold weather.

Emergency Heat and Aux Heat Are Not the Same

Many homeowners see "Aux Heat" on the thermostat and assume something is wrong. In most cases, auxiliary heat is a normal part of heat pump operation.

Aux heat turns on automatically when the heat pump needs help reaching the setpoint: during a sharp temperature drop, after you raise the thermostat several degrees at once, or while the system recovers from a defrost cycle. The heat pump keeps running while backup heat assists.

Emergency heat is different because you usually turn it on by hand. Once it is on, the outdoor heat pump is locked out and only the backup runs. That is why it costs more, especially with electric heat strips.

A short Aux Heat message on a cold morning is usually normal. Aux heat that runs all day, or emergency heat that has been on for days, deserves a closer look, unless a technician told you to use it while waiting for a repair.

When Should You Turn On Emergency Heat?

Use emergency heat when the outdoor unit is not working properly and you need reliable heat until a technician can check it. For example:

  • the outdoor unit is damaged, for example by a falling branch;
  • it makes loud grinding, banging or screeching noises;
  • it keeps shutting down or will not start after an electrical problem;
  • it is encased in ice that does not clear after defrost cycles.

It is also the right choice when a technician tells you to use it. During a diagnosis there are times when keeping the outdoor unit off protects the equipment while backup heat keeps the house comfortable.

Do not switch to emergency heat just because it is cold outside. Modern heat pumps are built to heat in cold weather, and a system sized correctly for a Washington home should carry most winter days on its own. Switching to emergency heat on every chilly day raises your bill without making the house warmer.

Why Emergency Heat Costs More

A heat pump is efficient because it moves heat instead of creating it. In mild weather it can deliver two to three times more heat than the electricity it uses. Electric heat strips turn each unit of electricity into one unit of heat, so they use substantially more power for the same warmth.

The exact difference depends on your equipment, insulation, thermostat habits, electric rates, outdoor temperature and whether your backup is electric or gas. There is no single dollar figure. What is consistent is that emergency heat with electric strips should be a short-term mode.

There is also a comfort trade-off. Backup heat may keep the house warm, but it does not fix whatever took the heat pump out of service. A system running only on backup can hide a failed part, an airflow problem, a control issue or an outdoor unit that needs repair.

What to Check Before Calling for Service

You do not need to troubleshoot refrigerant, wiring or internal parts. A few safe observations help the technician and may solve a simple problem.

1. The thermostat

Note whether it says Aux Heat or Emergency Heat, whether there is an error message and whether the house temperature is rising. If you just raised the setpoint several degrees, set it back to a steady temperature and give the system time. After a thermostat replacement, wrong backup-heat settings are a common cause; see thermostat problems and short cycling.

2. Filter and vents

Check the filter if you normally change it yourself. A badly clogged filter reduces airflow and makes the heat pump look weak. Make sure supply registers and returns are open and not covered by furniture or rugs.

3. The outdoor unit

From a safe distance: is it running? Is there visible damage? Is it making loud grinding, buzzing or repeated starting sounds? Some frost and a cloud of steam during defrost are normal; our guide to normal frost versus a service problem shows the difference. Do not chip ice off the coil, open electrical panels or keep resetting breakers. One reset is reasonable if there is no burning smell or visible damage. If it trips again, leave it off and call a technician.

When Backup Heat Runs Too Often

Sometimes emergency heat questions point to a bigger issue with the system itself. If backup heat runs frequently, rooms heat unevenly or winter bills have jumped, the cause may not be a single failed part. Equipment sizing, duct restrictions, thermostat setup, insulation and the type of backup heat all affect how a heat pump performs.

This matters most when replacing a system. A heat pump should not be chosen by square footage alone. Layout, windows, duct condition, electrical capacity and the actual heating load all deserve review, and a well-planned heat pump system comes with clear expectations about when aux heat will run. When Emergency Heat Needs Prompt Attention

Schedule a heat pump diagnostic promptly if emergency heat is the only way to keep the house warm, if the outdoor unit will not run or if the system keeps going into a fault. Turn the system off and call right away if you notice burning smells, smoke, sparking, damaged wiring, loud unusual noises or a breaker that will not stay on.

If backup heat is working safely, emergency mode is a practical bridge until service. Keep the thermostat at a steady temperature, avoid big setbacks and let the technician know emergency heat has been in use.

Use emergency heat when it is truly needed, then have the system checked so the heat pump can go back to doing the efficient everyday work it was installed for.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between aux heat and emergency heat?

Aux heat turns on automatically when the heat pump needs help, for example on a cold morning, after a big thermostat increase or during defrost, and the heat pump keeps running. Emergency heat is usually switched on by hand and shuts the outdoor heat pump off, so the house runs on backup heat alone.

Should I use emergency heat when it is cold outside?

No. Modern heat pumps are designed to heat in cold weather, and aux heat helps automatically when needed. Turn on emergency heat only when the outdoor unit is not working or a technician tells you to, and schedule a repair.

Is it bad to run emergency heat for a long time?

It will not usually harm the equipment, but with electric heat strips it can cost much more to run than the heat pump, and it hides whatever stopped the heat pump from working. Use it as a short bridge until service, not for the rest of the winter.
