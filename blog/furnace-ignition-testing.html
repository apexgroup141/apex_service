Furnace Ignition Testing: How Technicians Diagnose No-Heat Problems

See how technicians test the furnace startup sequence, ignition, flame sensing, and venting to diagnose a furnace that will not light or stay running.

A furnace can sound as if it is starting normally, then shut down before it delivers any heat. You may hear the thermostat click, the blower begin, or a short series of attempts to start. Furnace ignition testing is how a qualified technician determines where that startup sequence is stopping and whether the system is operating safely.

For Puget Sound homeowners, this is more than a comfort issue on a cold, wet morning. A gas furnace has to complete several safety checks before its burners are allowed to light. When one part of that sequence does not respond correctly, the furnace is designed to stop rather than continue operating under an unsafe condition. The right response is a careful diagnosis, not a guess or a part swap.

What furnace ignition testing actually checks

Modern gas furnaces do not simply receive a call for heat and ignite. They follow an ordered sequence controlled by the furnace control board. The exact design varies by equipment age and manufacturer, but a typical sequence begins when the thermostat requests heat.

The control board first verifies the required safety and control conditions before continuing the ignition sequence. It then starts the draft inducer, which moves combustion gases through the venting system. A pressure switch or pressure-sensing system verifies that the inducer and venting conditions are within the expected range before ignition continues. Only then does the igniter heat up or spark, the gas valve open, and the burners light. The flame sensor must then prove that a stable flame is present. After flame is established, the circulating blower normally starts following a delay. If the furnace does not receive that proof quickly enough, it closes the gas valve and may try again before entering a safety lockout.

A technician performing furnace ignition testing observes this sequence rather than treating every no-heat call as an igniter failure. The test may include checking the thermostat call, stored fault codes, voltage at specific points in the sequence, inducer operation, pressure-switch response, igniter condition, burner ignition, flame rectification signal (the flame-sensing signal used by the control board to confirm that flame is established), wiring, control-board commands, and venting conditions.

That order matters. A glowing igniter does not automatically mean the furnace is healthy. Likewise, an igniter that does not glow does not automatically mean the igniter itself has failed; the control board may be withholding power because an earlier safety condition was not satisfied. Likewise, a furnace that lights briefly may have a flame-sensing issue, a grounding problem, burner contamination, or another condition that needs to be identified before repairs are approved.

Signs your furnace may need ignition testing

A single interrupted heating cycle can occasionally result from a temporary power interruption or a thermostat setting issue. Repeated failures deserve attention, especially when outdoor temperatures are low. Common signs include a furnace that clicks but never lights, burners that light and go out within a few seconds, repeated startup attempts, or a system that runs the blower without producing warm air.

You may also notice that the furnace works intermittently. It heats the home one day, then fails the next. An igniter may be deteriorating, a pressure switch may respond inconsistently, or the flame-proving signal may be unstable. Intermittent operation is not something to ignore simply because heat eventually returns.

If you smell gas, leave the building and call the gas utility or emergency services from a safe location. Do not operate switches, create sparks, or attempt to relight the furnace. A gas odor is different from a routine ignition fault and should be treated accordingly.

Why the same symptom can have different causes

Homeowners often search for an answer such as “bad igniter” because that is a familiar furnace repair term. An igniter can fail, but it is only one part of a larger system. Replacing it without verifying the full sequence can leave the original problem unresolved.

For example, if the inducer motor does not create enough draft, the pressure switch may not close. The furnace then never sends power to the igniter because it has not confirmed safe venting. If the burners light but shut off almost immediately, the flame sensor may be dirty or failing, but the technician should also consider burner carryover, grounding, wiring connections, and the control board's response.

Venting concerns can also affect ignition. An obstructed intake or exhaust termination, a condensate-drainage issue on a condensing furnace, trapped water in the venting path, or damaged vent components can interrupt the safety sequence. These conditions should be evaluated professionally because combustion and venting work require the correct tools, training, and judgment.

Age changes the diagnostic approach as well. Older furnaces may use a standing pilot or intermittent pilot system rather than a hot-surface igniter. The underlying goal remains the same: verify that the furnace lights safely, proves flame, and continues operating as intended. The components and test procedure, however, are not identical.

What to expect during a professional diagnosis

A proper service visit should begin with what you have observed: whether the system is completely dead, whether it attempts to start, how often the issue occurs, and whether any status light is flashing on the furnace. Those details help, but the technician still needs to see the equipment operate.

The technician should inspect the furnace installation and run an operational test. That includes checking the startup sequence, looking for fault codes, confirming electrical connections and controls, and evaluating the ignition and flame-proving steps. Where the issue calls for it, they may inspect filters, condensate drainage, accessible venting, and airflow conditions that can contribute to furnace shutdowns.

The useful outcome is not just a diagnosis label. You should receive a plain-language explanation of what failed, what evidence supports that finding, and what the repair options are before work begins. If a part replacement is recommended, ask whether the rest of the system is operating correctly and whether there is evidence of another condition that contributed to the failure or could affect the repaired system.

After repair, the furnace should be run through its startup cycle again. Final testing confirms that it ignites, maintains flame, responds to the thermostat, and delivers heat without cycling into a fault. This verification is one of the details that separates a completed repair from a temporary restart.

Repair, maintenance, or replacement: the practical decision

Many ignition problems are repairable. A worn igniter, dirty flame sensor, damaged pressure switch, failed inducer motor, or loose electrical connection may have a straightforward repair path once the actual failure is confirmed. The right choice depends on the furnace's age, condition, repair history, safety, and the total cost of restoring reliable operation.

Replacement becomes a more reasonable conversation when an older furnace has repeated failures, significant corrosion, a major component issue, or repair costs that are high relative to the value and remaining life of the equipment. It also may make sense for homeowners already considering a heat pump upgrade, central air addition, ductwork changes, or an overall comfort improvement project.

There is no responsible one-answer recommendation for every home. A newer furnace with a clearly identified igniter problem should not be treated the same way as a 20-year-old unit with multiple unresolved concerns. Clear written options let you compare the immediate repair with longer-term equipment choices without pressure.

What homeowners can do safely before scheduling service

Start with the simple items that do not involve opening the furnace or handling gas and electrical components. Confirm that the thermostat is set to heat and set above the indoor temperature. Check that the furnace switch and circuit breaker have not been turned off. Make sure the return-air filter is not severely blocked. If your furnace has accessible exterior intake/exhaust terminations, confirm they are not obviously blocked by leaves, snow, or debris. Do not climb or dismantle venting to inspect them.

If the furnace has locked out, repeatedly cycling power to force another ignition attempt is not a solution. It can make intermittent issues harder to observe and does not correct the condition that caused the shutdown. Note any flashing diagnostic light pattern if it is visible through the furnace panel, then share that information with your service technician.

Do not remove panels, clean internal sensors, bypass safety switches, or attempt gas-related adjustments. The furnace safety sequence exists for a reason, and an accurate test requires more than getting the burners to light once.
