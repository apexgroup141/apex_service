How to Inspect HVAC Workmanship After Installation

You do not need to be a technician to tell a careful installation from a rushed one. Use this walk-through checklist before the crew leaves.

A new furnace, heat pump, air conditioner or mini-split can look finished long before the job is truly complete. Knowing how to inspect HVAC workmanship helps you look beyond the equipment cabinet and confirm the system was installed carefully, tested properly and explained clearly.

This guide is the after-installation companion to our residential HVAC installation guide and how to prepare your home for installation. A short checklist you can use on site is at the end.

Start With the Scope You Approved

Your written proposal is the baseline. Compare the completed work with the equipment model numbers, accessories, duct changes, electrical work, thermostat, permits and warranty information in the estimate. A proposal cannot predict every field adjustment, but significant changes should be discussed during the work, not discovered afterward. If you are still comparing bids, our HVAC proposal checklist shows what a complete scope should list.

Check the equipment labels indoors and out. They should match the quote or come with a written explanation for any substitution. For heat pumps and air conditioners, the indoor and outdoor components must be a matched, compatible pair; the wrong coil or air handler can hurt performance, efficiency and warranty coverage.

If the project included a new circuit, gas line changes, ductwork, condensate drainage or an equipment pad, confirm those items appear in the final paperwork. In Washington, electrical work typically requires a permit through L&I or the local jurisdiction, and many cities and counties in the Puget Sound area require mechanical permits for equipment replacement. Your contractor should explain what was required, who pulled the permit and what inspections remain; see our guide to HVAC permits and inspections in Washington.

Inspect the Workmanship You Can See

A visual check does not replace testing, but it shows whether the job was treated as a finished home improvement or a rushed swap.

Indoor equipment should be secure and serviceable

A furnace or air handler should sit level, be properly supported and leave room for service. Panels should fit, the filter should be easy to reach, and wiring should be protected and neatly routed, not draped across the cabinet.

For a furnace installation, look for properly connected venting and a clearly routed condensate drain on a high-efficiency model. Crushed vent pipe, unsupported sections, gaps or improvised connections deserve a question. Do not remove panels or test gas connections yourself; ask the installer to walk you through the safety checks. Our guide to furnace safety hazards explains what those checks protect against.

For a ductless mini-split, the indoor unit should be level, firmly mounted and placed so its filter and cover are accessible. Outside, the line-set cover should be straight and securely fastened, with a clean wall penetration sealed against weather and pests. That small detail says a lot about the rest of the job.

Outdoor equipment needs a solid, clear installation

The heat pump or condenser should sit level on a pad, stand or bracket and should not rock when lightly touched. In the Puget Sound area, heat pumps are often raised on a stand or bracket so rain, debris and defrost water can drain away from the unit. There should be clearance for airflow and service, with refrigerant line insulation intact and protected.

A quality crew restores the work area, hauls away old equipment and debris and does not leave sharp metal scraps, packaging or damaged landscaping. Some disturbance is unavoidable on larger projects; the question is whether it was addressed and the area left orderly.

Ductwork should look intentional, not improvised

Check visible sections in the garage, attic, basement or crawlspace access. Connections should be supported and sealed, not held with loose tape or sagging between supports. Flex duct should not be kinked or compressed, because restrictions starve rooms of air. If the project included duct changes, ask which runs changed and why. Our duct sealing guide shows what proper sealing looks like.

Ask for Proof of Startup and Performance Testing

The most important workmanship is not visible. Commissioning confirms that the equipment runs safely and delivers the airflow and heating or cooling the home needs.

Ask what was tested at startup. For a central system that usually includes thermostat operation, temperature rise or drop, static pressure, blower setup, drain operation, electrical readings, refrigerant charge verification where applicable and airflow adjustments. Our HVAC commissioning guide lists each test and why it matters.

A heat pump installed on a mild fall day cannot be tested under the load of a January cold snap or a July heat wave. That does not mean testing should be skipped. The contractor should explain what was verified that day, what conditions limited testing and what follow-up makes sense if a concern appears later.

Keep the final invoice, equipment model and serial numbers, permit records, warranty registration, operating instructions and any test notes. You will need them for service, warranty claims, rebates and a future home sale; see HVAC warranty coverage for what registration protects.

Red Flags, Without Judging Too Quickly

Not every unfamiliar detail is a defect. A drain may take a new route because the old one was wrong; an outdoor unit may move to meet clearances or reduce noise near a bedroom. The concern is not that the work looks different. It is when no one can explain why.

Call promptly about water around indoor equipment, repeated error codes, unusual vibration, rooms with much worse airflow, a thermostat that does not behave as explained or a system that short-cycles. Also ask about loose panels, torn line insulation, disconnected ductwork or an installer who left without demonstrating basic operation. Long, steady run times on a new heat pump are normal; frequent short cycles are not.

A good contractor separates a normal adjustment period from an issue that needs a return visit. Airflow balancing in an older home can take more than one visit; our airflow balancing guide explains why.

Final Walk-Through Checklist

Before the crew leaves, take ten minutes with the person who installed the system and confirm:

  • Installed equipment matches the model numbers in your proposal, or changes were explained in writing.
  • Indoor and outdoor units are level, secure and accessible for service.
  • Line sets, wiring, venting and drains are neat, supported and sealed where they pass through walls.
  • Visible ductwork is supported, sealed and free of kinks.
  • You know what startup tests were done and have the results or notes.
  • You can operate the thermostat or mini-split remote for heat, cool and fan.
  • You know where the filter is, its size and how often to check it.
  • You know where disconnects, drains and shutoffs are.
  • You have the invoice, warranty details and permit information, and know which inspections remain.
  • You know who to call with a startup question.

Frequently Asked Questions

What documents should I get after an HVAC installation?

Keep the final invoice, equipment model and serial numbers, permit records if a permit was required, warranty registration details, operating instructions and any startup or commissioning notes. You will need them for service, warranty claims, rebates and a future home sale.

Is it normal for a new heat pump to run for long periods?

Yes. Heat pumps, especially inverter models, are designed to run longer at lower output than a furnace. Long, quiet run times with steady temperatures are normal. Frequent short on-off cycles, error codes or water around indoor equipment are reasons to call the installer.

Should I check gas or electrical connections myself?

No. Do not open equipment panels or test gas or electrical connections. Ask the installer to explain the safety checks completed at startup and to show you the results.
