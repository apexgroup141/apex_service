Why Heat Pumps Are a Great Fit for the Puget Sound Climate

Western Washington's mild marine climate can be a great match for modern heat pumps. Learn why local temperatures, humidity, defrost, sizing and variable-capacity operation all matter.

Heat pumps have become an increasingly popular option for heating and cooling homes across Western Washington. But their popularity is not only about new technology, energy incentives, or changing HVAC trends.

The climate around Puget Sound is actually well suited to modern air-source heat pump systems.

Much of Western Washington — including King, Pierce, Snohomish, and Thurston Counties — is classified as Climate Zone 4C, Marine under the Washington State Energy Code.

Winters here are generally milder than in many inland parts of the United States, while periods of extreme cold are relatively limited.

That does not mean every heat pump is automatically the right choice for every home. Equipment selection, sizing, ductwork, airflow, installation quality, and the characteristics of the house still matter.

But for many homes in the Puget Sound region, the local climate gives a properly selected heat pump very favorable operating conditions for much of the heating season.

Why Outdoor Temperature Matters for Heat Pumps

An air-source heat pump does not create heat the same way a gas furnace or electric resistance heater does.

Instead, it transfers heat from the outdoor air into the home.

Even when the outdoor air feels cold, it still contains thermal energy that a heat pump can use.

As outdoor temperatures fall, however, heating capacity and efficiency can change depending on the specific equipment.

This is why climate matters when selecting a heat pump.

In regions that experience long periods of severe cold, low-temperature performance becomes especially important.

Around Puget Sound, a heat pump will typically spend much of the heating season operating at temperatures that are considerably less extreme.

That is one of the reasons air-source heat pumps can be such a good match for Western Washington.

Modern cold-climate heat pump technology has also improved significantly, with systems designed to retain useful heating capacity at much lower outdoor temperatures than older equipment.

Seattle, Tacoma, Bellevue and Olympia Are Not Spokane

When discussing heat pumps in “Washington State,” it is important to remember that Washington has very different climate regions.

The Cascade Mountains divide the state into areas with very different heating and cooling conditions.

Much of Western Washington has a marine climate with relatively mild, wet winters.

Many areas east of the Cascades are classified as Climate Zone 5B, which means colder and drier conditions. Spokane, for example, is in Zone 5B, while King, Pierce, Snohomish and Thurston Counties are in Zone 4C.

That means an HVAC system for a home in Bellevue or Tacoma should not necessarily be selected using the same assumptions as a system for a home in Spokane.

The correct approach is to consider the actual heating and cooling loads of the home and the local outdoor design conditions.

Does Every Puget Sound Home Need a Cold-Climate Heat Pump?

Not necessarily.

Cold-climate heat pumps are designed to provide stronger heating performance at low outdoor temperatures.

That capability can be extremely valuable in colder climates.

But in Puget Sound, the better question is not:

“Should I buy the most powerful cold-climate heat pump available?”

The better question is:

“How much heating capacity does this home actually need at the local winter design temperature?”

For some homes, equipment with very strong low-temperature capacity makes excellent sense.

For others, a different model may meet the home's heating requirements perfectly well without paying for performance that is rarely needed.

Factors such as insulation, windows, air leakage, ductwork and the actual heating load of the building should all be considered.

Wet Puget Sound Winters and Defrost Mode

Western Washington has another climate characteristic that affects heat pump operation: winters are often both cool and humid.

When a heat pump operates in heating mode, the outdoor coil can become colder than the surrounding air.

Under certain temperature and humidity conditions, moisture can collect on the coil and freeze.

A light layer of frost is normal.

To remove it, the heat pump periodically enters defrost mode.

During a defrost cycle, a homeowner may notice:

  • the outdoor fan temporarily stopping
  • a change in system sound
  • steam rising from the outdoor unit
  • water around the unit after the cycle finishes
  • a temporary change in supply-air temperature

These signs do not automatically indicate a problem.

For heat pumps operating through a cool, damp Puget Sound winter, periodic defrost is a normal part of operation.

A problem may exist if heavy ice continues building up, the coil does not clear properly, or the unit remains covered in ice. If icing persists or the unit cannot complete defrost normally, professional heat pump service may be appropriate.

We will cover heat pump defrost in more detail in a separate HVAC guide.

One System Can Provide Both Heating and Cooling

Another reason heat pumps are attractive in Western Washington is that one system can provide both heating and air conditioning.

Historically, many homes in the Puget Sound region were built with heating systems but without central air conditioning.

A heat pump can address both needs:

Heating in winter.
Cooling in summer.

For a homeowner already considering replacement or modernization of an HVAC system, this can make a heat pump an attractive option.

Instead of installing only an air conditioner while continuing to rely entirely on an older heating system, a heat pump can potentially provide both functions.

That does not mean it is automatically the best choice in every home. Existing equipment, ductwork, budget and homeowner goals should still be evaluated.

Why Variable-Speed Heat Pumps Work Well in a Mild Climate

Many modern heat pumps use inverter-driven or variable-capacity compressors.

Rather than operating only at full output and then shutting off, these systems can adjust their capacity as the home's heating or cooling demand changes.

During mild weather, the home may need only a fraction of the system's maximum output.

A variable-capacity heat pump can operate for longer periods at lower capacity instead of repeatedly starting and stopping.

Homeowners sometimes notice this after upgrading:

“Why does my new heat pump run for so long?”

Longer run times are not necessarily a sign of excessive energy use or a malfunction.

For a properly selected inverter heat pump, long low-capacity cycles can be completely normal.

This type of operation can help provide:

  • more consistent indoor temperatures
  • fewer compressor starts and stops
  • more even airflow
  • quieter operation
  • efficient performance during partial-load conditions

Because Puget Sound spends a large part of the heating season at temperatures well above the coldest winter design conditions, variable-capacity operation can be particularly useful.

A Good Climate Cannot Fix Poor HVAC Design

Even in a climate that is well suited to heat pumps, poor system design can result in poor performance.

Choosing HVAC equipment based only on the square footage of the home or the size of the previous furnace is not enough.

Important factors include:

  • insulation levels
  • windows and doors
  • air leakage
  • ceiling height
  • exterior wall area
  • orientation and solar exposure
  • duct sizing
  • return-air capacity
  • airflow
  • static pressure
  • equipment location
  • heating load
  • cooling load

A larger heat pump is not automatically a better heat pump.

Likewise, an undersized system may not provide enough heating capacity during the coldest conditions.

Correct equipment selection should be based on the home itself.

Your Existing Heating System Matters Too

Not every homeowner in Washington is starting with the same heating system.

One home may currently use a natural gas furnace.

Another may have electric resistance heating.

Another may use electric baseboards.

Homes in rural areas may use propane or other heating fuels.

The benefits and economics of installing a heat pump can therefore be very different from one property to another.

For example, replacing electric resistance heating with a heat pump is a very different situation from replacing a relatively new high-efficiency gas furnace.

Puget Sound Energy currently offers heating incentives for several qualifying heat pump applications, including certain electric-resistance-to-heat-pump conversions and hybrid heat pump installations.

Programs and eligibility can change, so homeowners should verify current requirements before making an equipment decision.

A rebate can help make a project more attractive, but it should not be the only reason to select an HVAC system.

The system still needs to be right for the home.

Are Heat Pumps a Good Choice for Puget Sound?

For many homes, yes.

The relatively mild marine climate of Western Washington provides favorable conditions for modern air-source heat pumps during much of the year.

The ability to provide both heating and cooling from one system is also particularly useful in a region where many older homes were originally built without central air conditioning.

But the equipment itself is only part of the system.

Good results also depend on:

  • correct sizing
  • proper duct design
  • adequate airflow
  • quality installation
  • correct refrigerant setup
  • proper controls
  • suitable outdoor-unit placement
  • matching the equipment to the specific home

So the most useful question is not simply:

“Do heat pumps work well in Puget Sound?”

In many cases, they do.

The better question is:

“Which heat pump and system configuration are right for this particular home?”

That answer should come from evaluating the home, the existing HVAC system and the homeowner's comfort goals before selecting equipment.
