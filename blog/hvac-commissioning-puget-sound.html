HVAC Commissioning Guide for Puget Sound Homes

Understand the testing, adjustments and documentation that verify a new HVAC system is working as designed.

A new furnace, heat pump, air conditioner, or mini-split is only as good as the way it performs in your home.

This HVAC commissioning guide explains the final step that turns an installation into a working comfort system: measuring, testing, adjusting, and documenting the equipment after it is installed.

For homeowners, commissioning should not feel like an extra mystery charge or a quick look at a thermostat before the crew leaves.

It is the process that verifies your new system is delivering the airflow, heating, cooling, drainage, controls, and safety performance it was designed to provide.

In the Puget Sound area, where many homes are moving from older gas equipment to heat pumps, those details can make a real difference in comfort and operating costs.

What HVAC Commissioning Means in a Home

HVAC commissioning is a structured performance check completed after installation or major system replacement.

The technician confirms that the equipment is installed according to manufacturer requirements, applicable code, and the intended system design.

Then the system is tested under operating conditions and startup adjustments are made where appropriate.

It is different from simply turning the unit on.

A system can produce warm or cool air and still have poor airflow, incorrect refrigerant charge, a drainage issue, duct leakage, improper control settings, or uneven temperatures from room to room.

Those issues may not cause an immediate breakdown, but they can shorten equipment life and leave parts of the home uncomfortable.

Commissioning also differs from routine maintenance.

Maintenance keeps an existing system clean and operating over time.

Commissioning establishes a verified baseline at the beginning, which makes future service easier and gives the homeowner a clearer record of how the system was set up.

Why It Matters Before the Installer Leaves

Most homeowners notice HVAC problems when a bedroom stays cold, a second floor overheats, or the system runs longer than expected.

Those symptoms often trace back to design or installation details, not necessarily a defective piece of equipment.

A correctly sized heat pump can still struggle if the return duct is too restrictive.

A high-efficiency furnace can underperform when blower settings are not matched to the duct system.

A ductless mini-split can drain poorly if its condensate routing is not installed correctly.

Commissioning is where these details are checked instead of assumed.

The process also helps protect the equipment investment.

Manufacturers may require specific installation procedures to support warranty coverage.

Proper electrical connections, refrigerant line practices, combustion setup on gas equipment, and documented startup readings are not paperwork for its own sake.

They help show that the equipment was installed and tested as intended.

HVAC Commissioning Guide: What Should Be Tested

The exact test list depends on the equipment.

A gas furnace needs combustion and venting checks that do not apply to an all-electric heat pump.

A ductless system has no central ductwork to balance, while a ducted system requires careful attention to static pressure and airflow.

Still, a complete residential commissioning process generally covers several core areas.

Equipment Installation and Electrical Checks

The technician first verifies the basic installation.

That includes secure mounting, required service clearances, correct disconnects, properly sized electrical protection, condensate connections, filter access, and accessible shutoff valves where applicable.

Outdoor heat-pump and air-conditioning units should be level, stable, and positioned so they can drain and defrost properly.

For indoor equipment, the installer should confirm that supply and return connections are sealed, the filter rack fits correctly, and the cabinet is not pulling unfiltered air from an attic, crawlspace, garage, or mechanical room.

Small gaps can introduce dust or moisture and affect system performance.

Airflow and Duct Performance

Airflow is one of the most overlooked parts of residential HVAC work.

Your equipment needs an appropriate volume of air moving across its indoor coil or heat exchanger.

Too little airflow can contribute to noisy operation, frozen coils, high static pressure, reduced capacity, and premature blower wear.

Too much airflow can create drafts and noise and, during cooling, may reduce moisture removal when humidity control is needed.

For a ducted system, testing may include total external static pressure, blower settings, supply and return temperature readings, and airflow adjustments.

If certain rooms have always been hotter or colder than the rest of the home, the technician may also review damper positions, register placement, return-air paths, and whether the duct layout can reasonably support the desired improvement.

Not every comfort problem can be solved with a damper adjustment.

A poorly insulated bonus room, an undersized return, or a problematic duct run may require a separate ductwork modification.

A dependable contractor should explain that difference clearly rather than promise that a new thermostat will fix every room.

Refrigerant, Heating, and Cooling Operation

Heat pumps and central air conditioners require more than a quick check for cold air.

The technician verifies refrigerant circuit operation using manufacturer procedures and the conditions present during testing.

Depending on the system, this may include operating pressures, temperatures, superheat, subcooling, and variable-speed performance data where applicable.

Airflow should be verified before refrigerant charge is evaluated because incorrect airflow can distort refrigerant readings and contribute to an incorrect diagnosis.

Refrigerant charge cannot be guessed.

Too little or too much refrigerant can reduce capacity, increase energy use, and stress the compressor.

Modern inverter-driven heat pumps are especially dependent on correct installation practices, evacuation procedures, line-set sizing, and control configuration.

During heating and cooling tests, the contractor should also confirm that the thermostat or system controller is calling for the correct mode, indoor and outdoor units communicate properly, and auxiliary or backup heat operates when required.

In Washington, backup heat settings deserve special attention because they affect cold-weather comfort and electric use.

Safety Checks for Gas and Electrical Systems

If your home has a gas furnace, startup safety testing should include appropriate combustion checks, gas pressure verification according to manufacturer requirements and applicable installation procedures, venting inspection, and confirmation that safety controls operate correctly.

Carbon monoxide concerns should always be taken seriously.

The furnace cabinet, vent piping, condensate system on condensing furnaces, and combustion-air arrangement all need to be evaluated as part of a proper startup.

Electrical checks matter for every system type.

Loose connections, incorrect voltage, poor grounding, or improperly matched electrical protection can create safety issues or equipment failures that may initially look like manufacturer defects.

These checks are particularly valuable during replacements, when older wiring may not match the requirements of new equipment.

What Homeowners Should Receive at Completion

You do not need to understand every pressure reading or airflow calculation, but you should leave the project with a clear explanation of what was installed and how it was verified.

A professional handoff should include equipment model information, basic operating instructions, filter size and replacement schedule, warranty information, and any recommended follow-up work.

For a larger project, ask for the final settings or startup documentation that applies to your system.

This may include airflow measurements, static pressure, temperature rise for a furnace, refrigerant commissioning data, or combustion results.

The records can be useful if a warranty issue arises later or another technician services the system.

The contractor should also show you how to use the thermostat or controls.

With heat pumps, that conversation should cover normal operating sounds, defrost cycles, appropriate thermostat operation, filter care, and when to call for service.

A system that is working normally can sound and behave differently from an older furnace or air conditioner.

Questions Worth Asking Your HVAC Contractor

Before approving an installation, it is reasonable to ask how performance testing will be handled after the equipment is in place.

Ask whether the proposal includes commissioning, whether duct airflow will be evaluated, and what documentation you will receive at the end.

For a heat-pump project, ask how the contractor will verify operation and backup heat settings. The same questions apply when planning heat pump installation in Puyallup.

For a furnace replacement, ask whether combustion and temperature rise will be tested.

If your home has rooms with known comfort problems, ask whether the proposed scope addresses the underlying airflow or duct issue rather than only replacing the equipment.

Clear answers matter.

The person who evaluated the home should understand the plan well enough to explain what will be tested, what limitations exist, and what happens if testing identifies an issue outside the original scope.

That direct accountability helps prevent surprises after installation day.

When Commissioning May Need a Follow-Up Visit

Some performance conditions cannot be fully evaluated on the day of installation.

Cooling testing may be limited during cold outdoor weather, while a mild day may not show how a heat pump performs under winter load.

New-construction homes can also change after occupants move in, furniture blocks registers, or final interior doors affect return-air circulation.

A follow-up is also appropriate when duct modifications, zoning controls, or major airflow balancing are part of the project.

The goal is not to claim perfection in every room.

It is to verify the system, document the results, and make practical adjustments based on how the home actually operates.

When you are comparing HVAC proposals, look beyond the equipment name and efficiency rating.

Ask who will inspect the home, who will perform the installation, and how they will prove the system is working correctly.

A careful final test is one of the clearest signs that comfort, safety, and long-term operating costs were considered from the start.
