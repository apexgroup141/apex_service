Heat Pump vs. Baseboard Heat for Puget Sound Homes

Many Puget Sound homes still heat with electric baseboards. Here is how a heat pump compares on monthly cost, comfort, cooling and winter performance, and when baseboards are still the right call.

A baseboard heater feels like the simplest answer when a room is cold: turn the dial, wait a few minutes and feel heat along the wall. But when homeowners compare a heat pump vs. baseboard heat, the bigger question is how to make the whole home comfortable without taking on the wrong project or living with high winter electric bills.

Electric baseboards are common in Puget Sound homes built in the 1960s through the 1980s, in many condos and in additions and finished basements across Tacoma, Lakewood, Federal Way and Olympia. For most of these homes, a heat pump is the stronger long-term heating and cooling option. Baseboards are not obsolete, though: they still make sense in a small addition, a rarely used room or a home where a full upgrade is not the right fit.

The Core Difference

Electric baseboards create heat through electric resistance. Electricity flows through an element, the element gets hot and warms the air around it. Every unit of heat costs one unit of electricity.

A heat pump moves heat instead of making it. Even when outdoor air feels cold, a modern heat pump extracts usable heat from it and moves it inside. In typical Western Washington winter weather, that means two to three units of heat for each unit of electricity, sometimes more on mild days.

The practical result: baseboards usually cost less to install, while a properly designed heat pump usually costs much less to operate. A heat pump also cools in summer. Baseboards only heat.

Upfront Cost Is Only One Part of the Decision

Replacing one failed baseboard heater is relatively inexpensive if the circuit, wiring and thermostat are in good condition. Adding baseboards can also make sense where extending ductwork would be disruptive.

A heat pump is a larger investment. In a baseboard home with no ducts, the usual path is a ductless mini-split: an outdoor unit, one or more indoor units, refrigerant lines, condensate drainage, electrical work and careful placement. If the home has ductwork, a ducted heat pump may work, but the ducts must be checked first; see can ducts support heat pumps.

Compare total cost over time, not just the installation price. If baseboards heat a whole house, winter electric use adds up quickly; a heat pump can cut that substantially while adding cooling and better comfort.

Utility rebates can change the numbers. Puget Sound Energy, Tacoma Power and other local utilities have offered incentives for replacing electric resistance heat with a heat pump, but programs, amounts and rules change, so confirm current terms before you decide. Note that the federal residential heat pump tax credit ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025. Comfort Is Where the Difference Shows

Baseboards heat the perimeter of each room. They are quiet and offer room-by-room control, but they often leave uneven temperatures in open floor plans, rooms with high ceilings or poorly insulated exterior walls, and rooms far from the thermostat. Furniture, curtains and rugs must stay clear for safe heat circulation.

A ductless indoor unit moves conditioned air across the room, while a ducted system supplies air through registers. Design matters: an oversized unit cycles too often, and an undersized or poorly placed unit leaves bedrooms or distant rooms cool.

A heat pump does not mean every room feels identical. Closed doors, multi-level layouts and isolated bedrooms affect airflow. Some homes need several indoor zones, a ducted solution, or a hybrid that keeps a baseboard in one hard-to-reach room. The goal is to match the system to how the home actually works, not to install the most equipment.

Summer cooling changes the equation

In Tacoma, Puyallup, Seattle and nearby communities, summers are mild most days, but heat waves make upstairs and west-facing rooms uncomfortable, and recent summers have had more of them. Baseboards offer nothing for that. A heat pump cools with the same equipment, which is a major advantage for homeowners currently relying on window units or portable fans.

Winter Performance in Western Washington

Western Washington’s moderate winters suit modern heat pumps well, which we cover in why heat pumps work well in Puget Sound. Older heat pumps earned a reputation for weak cold-weather output; current cold-climate equipment keeps useful capacity at temperatures well below what the Tacoma and Seattle lowlands usually see.

During cold periods a heat pump runs longer than a furnace because it delivers steady, lower-temperature heat rather than short blasts of hot air. That is normal. You may also see the outdoor unit pause to defrost, which our defrost cycle guide explains.

Some homes need supplemental heat for the coldest days or a specific room. That backup can be electric heat in an air handler, a few existing baseboards or another planned source. The right plan comes from a load calculation and an on-site assessment, not a rule of thumb based on square footage.

When Baseboards Still Make Sense

Baseboard heat is reasonable when the scope is small and the room has a clear, occasional-use purpose: a small enclosed addition, a guest room or an isolated space that does not justify extending a central system. It also works as targeted backup when the main system handles most of the house but one room has unusual heat loss.

Before adding more baseboard capacity, check insulation, air leakage, windows and airflow. A cold room is not always an equipment problem. And if a home has baseboards throughout, replacing failed units one by one fixes a local failure without changing the operating-cost picture. If you plan to stay for years, price a heat pump option alongside the like-for-like replacement. For detached spaces, see our garage heating options.

Installation Details That Should Not Be Skipped

A heat pump project is not an equipment swap. Indoor and outdoor locations affect noise, service access, drainage, appearance and performance. Line sets should be neat and protected. Electrical capacity must be evaluated; in many baseboard homes, removing some resistance heat frees capacity for the heat pump, but that needs to be calculated, not assumed. See HVAC electrical requirements.

Baseboard work needs care too: each heater must be sized for the room and connected to proper controls and circuits, and adding high-wattage resistance heat can strain panel capacity in older homes.

Either way, you should receive a written scope before approving the work: equipment, electrical or duct changes, how permits are handled and what testing happens at the end. For a full baseboard-to-heat-pump conversion plan, read our heat pump retrofit guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much cheaper is a heat pump to run than baseboard heat?

Baseboards turn one unit of electricity into one unit of heat. In typical Western Washington winter weather, a modern heat pump often delivers two to three units of heat for the same electricity, so heating cost for the same space is commonly cut by roughly half or more. Actual savings depend on the home, the equipment, electricity rates and how the system is used.

Do I have to remove my baseboards when I install a heat pump?

No. Many homeowners keep baseboards in rooms the heat pump does not reach well, such as a closed bathroom or a small back bedroom, and use them as backup on the coldest days. The heat pump becomes the main heat source.

Will a heat pump keep my house warm in a Puget Sound cold snap?

A correctly sized modern heat pump handles typical Western Washington winters well. For the coldest days, the design should include a plan for backup heat, which may simply be existing baseboards or electric heat in an air handler.
